# Hook Strategies

Capture attention, establish what the content is about, and create a compelling reason to keep watching.

## Contents

- Three hook channels
- Timing guidelines

## Three Types of Hooks

All active channels should be aligned and point to the same subject and central question. They can complement one another without repeating identical information.

- **Visual Hook:** What is shown on screen.
- **Spoken Hook:** What is said verbally by the creator.
- **Text Hook:** The text written over the visuals.

## Timing Guidelines

- **Short Form:** Hook the viewer within the first **2–3 seconds**.
- **Long Form (YouTube):** Use **1–2 minutes** for the full hook-and-intro sequence while earning attention from the first seconds.

Treat these as working windows, not time to fill. Move into the substance once the opening has done its job.

## Hook Writing Checklist

- **Step 1: Choose the Subject (North Star)** — Define the single topic the video focuses on.
- **Step 2: Choose the Question** — Decide what the audience should wonder after the hook. Use curiosity, emotion, or surprise to lead into the next part.
- **Step 3: Plan the Visual** — Decide what appears on screen and how it supports the spoken hook.
- **Step 4: Write the Spoken Hook** — Use 1–3 sentences with as few words as possible.
  - Write in active, direct voice: “The dog chased the cat,” not “The cat was chased by the dog.”
  - Aim for sixth-grade reading clarity.
  - Be punchy and concise; remove filler.
- **Step 5: Check One Subject, One Question**
  - Does the hook unmistakably communicate one subject?
  - Does it unmistakably trigger one central question in the audience?
  - If either answer is no, return to Step 4.
- **Step 6: Align the Edit** — Build the visuals, add the text hook, and align them with the spoken hook.
- **Step 7: Last-Chance Audit**
  - Is the intended subject clear?
  - Is the intended question clear?
  - Is the overall presentation visually interesting?
  - Am I curious enough to keep watching—especially the next sentence?

For experiential content, interpret “one central question” as the dominant anticipation or entry experience when an explicit question would be inappropriate.

## Hook Line Sinker Framework (Curiosity Loop)

A 3-step hook formula that establishes context, interrupts the audience's expectation, and introduces a new perspective that opens a Curiosity Loop.

**Context Lean → Scroll-Stopping Interjection → Contrarian Snapback**

### Step 1: Context Lean

- Immediately make it crystal clear what the video is about and what the audience should expect. Keep **one subject and one central question** in focus.
- Make them lean in by:
  - Establishing common ground—be relatable, speak from their perspective, or talk to your younger self.
  - Connecting to a benefit they want or a pain point they have.
  - Using a metaphor to simplify a complex idea.
  - Sharing something genuinely interesting, surprising, or controversial.

### Step 2: Scroll-Stopping Interjection — “The Pull”

- **The Stun Gun.** Interrupt the expected direction of the setup. Make the audience feel there is more to the story. **This is the setup for the Snapback.**
- **Four ways to create the Pull:**
  - **Taboo Pull:** Introduce a controversial or slightly uncomfortable statement.
  - **Dark / Reveal Pull:** Expose something hidden or unknown to the audience.
  - **Contradiction Pull:** Challenge something they already believe to be true.
  - **Proof Pull:** Present factual evidence or a compelling statistic that backs up a statement you've made. Visually showing the evidence strengthens your case and helps set up the Snapback.
- Use contrasting words such as **“but,” “however,” or “yet”** to signal the shift. The words support the contrast; they do not create it on their own.
- **Storytelling Secret Sauce — A vs. B:**
  - **A:** What the audience already believes.
  - **B:** An alternative you're suggesting that solves their pain point **faster, better, or cheaper**.
  - Contrast is the distance between that existing belief and your alternative perspective.
- **Two ways to communicate contrast:**
  - **Stated Contrast:** Explicitly explain both A and B.
  - **Implied Contrast:** State B without explaining A because the baseline belief is already understood.
- Acknowledge and confirm their baseline expectation, then signal something new that gives them a reason to anticipate more.

### Step 3: Contrarian Snapback

- Deliver the unexpected perspective that pulls the audience in a different or opposite direction from the initial Context Lean.
- Make the shift surprising enough to create curiosity, but grounded in what the content can deliver.
- Open a compelling question that leads into the next part of the video. The audience should want to hear the next sentence—not simply react to the shock.

**The Pull interrupts the expectation; the Snapback reveals the new direction.**

## The Desire Hook Framework (Sales)

Hook viewers by teasing a desire they already have through a relatable character they see themselves in. Paint the dream outcome, then offer the method behind it—that's what the audience wants.

### Two Things Matter

- **Relatable Character** — Someone the audience can see themselves in:
  - You, the creator.
  - Them, the viewer.
  - Another person whose circumstances feel relevant to them.
- **Dream Outcome** — Something the character has achieved or is going to attempt.
  - Connect to a pain point the audience wants solved or an outcome they already want.
  - **Relatability Check:** The audience will immediately assess how relevant or relatable the character who achieved that outcome is. If they cannot relate, they may click off. If they can, the outcome feels achievable for them.

### Hook Structure

**Dream Outcome → Creator/Character → Relatable Condition**

- Establish the desired outcome and why it feels relevant within the **first two lines**.
- The relatable condition can be the method, starting point, or constraint that connects the character's situation to the audience.

### Hook Templates by Perspective

#### Creator's Perspective

- **Template 1: About Me — Looking Back:** “I just accomplished [dream outcome] using [method].”
- **Template 2: If I — Looking Forward:** “If I wanted to accomplish [dream outcome], I would use [method].”

#### Viewer's Perspective

- **Template 3: To You:** “If you're trying to achieve [dream outcome], use [relatable method].”
- **Template 4: Can You — Possibility or Challenge:** “Is it possible to achieve [dream outcome] under [relatable conditions]?”

#### Third-Party Perspective

- **Template 5: He/She Just Did:** “[Character] just achieved [dream outcome] under [relatable conditions].”

### Make the Hook Declarative

- Where it strengthens the opening, replace conditional phrasing with a direct statement.
- Instead of “If you want to improve your storytelling, try this,” use “This is how you improve your storytelling.”
- Keep a question when the uncertainty is what makes the hook compelling, and never frame a hypothetical result as something already achieved.

## Hook Stacking Framework (Problem → Solution)

Build an opening by layering three connected beats: introduce a relevant problem, explain what is not working, and introduce the solution.

**Angle → Current State → Solution**

### Step 1: Angle — Introduce the Problem

- Choose the specific perspective that makes the problem relevant to the audience.
- Establish one clear subject and a recognizable pain point, obstacle, or unmet goal.
- Make the viewer understand why this matters to them.

### Step 2: Current State — Explain What Is Not Working

- Identify the current approach, assumption, or situation keeping the problem unresolved.
- Add a specific limitation or explanation rather than repeating the opening complaint.
- Create contrast between where the audience is and the change they need.

### Step 3: Solution — Introduce the Way Forward

- Offer the method, insight, or alternative that addresses the problem.
- In an opening, preview enough of the solution to provide value and make the next explanation worth watching.
- In a very short video, deliver the solution directly when it can be explained clearly.

### Example

- **Angle:** “You want to make more videos without losing time to create.”
- **Current State:** “But treating every post as a brand-new project means starting from zero each time.”
- **Solution:** “Start with one creative session and find three distinct stories inside it.”

### Best Fit

Problem-led explainers, advice, process breakdowns, and persuasive content where recognizing the audience’s situation creates the initial interest.

### Key Rule

Each layer must advance the opening: identify the problem, explain what is failing, then introduce a useful way forward. Keep all three connected to one subject and one central question or promise.

### Relationship to Other Methods

- **Hook Line Sinker:** Creates curiosity through an expectation interruption and unexpected perspective. Hook Stacking can work through straightforward diagnosis and solution.
- **Desire Hook:** Leads with a dream outcome and relatable character or condition. Hook Stacking leads with the problem and current obstacle.
- **Supporting techniques:** Lead with Existing Interest, A vs. B Contrast, and Compress Speed to Value can strengthen the individual beats.

Primarily structures the opening. It can also organize a very short problem–solution video; longer pieces need further development, demonstration, and payoff.

## Supporting Hook Tips

### Visual Hooks

- Use a concise on-screen title to establish context.
- Catch attention with initial motion, a compelling action, or a stunning visual.
- Pair the visual with interesting spoken words that pull the viewer in, keeping the visual, spoken, and text hooks aligned around the same idea.

### Idea Collision (Contrast)

- Pair two seemingly incompatible ideas, identities, worlds, or situations to create immediate contrast and curiosity.
- Examples: “Religious Leader vs. Drunken Man” and “Jesus and the Woman at the Well.”
- The story must eventually explain or resolve the relationship between them.

### Lead with Existing Interest

- People care about things they are already interested in.
- Lead with a desired benefit, a recognizable pain point, or new information that goes beyond what they already know.

### Cult Hopping

- Wrap an unfamiliar idea in something familiar.
- Use a known brand, celebrity, movement, or cultural reference as a metaphor to help the audience connect with your point.

### Compress Speed to Value

- Deliver some value within the hook or immediately after it.
- Alternate **Context → Value → Context → Value**, giving the audience progress and a reason to keep watching.
- Keep people entertained and drawn to the next point—they should feel like it's impossible to click away.

### Make Hook Sentences Staccato

- Use short, punchy sentences that force clarity.
- Remove filler and unnecessary setup. Each sentence should make the audience want to hear the next.
